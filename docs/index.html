What these books give

At the centre of these books is the human being. Not the lowest rung beneath the angels, as has been taught for centuries, but, in the words of The Devil's Scribe Is Always Right, “the only being to whom all the others turn when they need what they themselves cannot do”. The author wants readers to remember who they are: the spirit of our Father is in them, and they pay their debt not in the sacrifices that Enoch taught people to offer, but in “the only currency for which the chancery has no tariff—love”.

About the author

Albert Lashev is the author of investigative books on early Christianity, the papacy and power in European history. He divides his time between Spain and Russia and writes in Russian; his books are also published in English, German, Spanish, French and Italian.

The author reads his sources word for word, marks every claim as document, reconstruction or tradition, names the weak points of his own theories himself and shows where they can be checked. The first book of each cycle is available free of charge.

The Greatest Substitution

The cycle The Greatest Substitution is about the man the world knows as Jesus Christ (in these books, Radomir) and about those who stood beside him: Mary Magdalene, John, Philip and Paul. Among the cycle's conclusions: supersubstantial bread (panem supersubstantialem) in the Lord's Prayer in place of the familiar daily bread; two chapters of the Apocalypse transposed, after which the book answers a different question; and the observation that half of the New Testament was written by a man who never met his teacher.   • Meet Jesus Christ
    About the book

    “This story does not begin in a manger in Bethlehem, but under the starry sky of Cappadocia. It does not continue on the roads of Galilee, but on the turbulent waters of the Bosphorus. And it does not end in Jerusalem, but at the heart of a future empire.”

    This book is neither a theological treatise nor an atheist polemic. It is an investigation. It draws on 127 canonical and apocryphal texts, archaeological data, linguistic expertise and comparative religious studies — and reconstructs the true biography of a man whom the whole world knows, yet almost no one truly knows.

    Christ was born not in Bethlehem but in Cappadocia. He preached not in Jerusalem but in Byzantium. He was not executed — he was rescued. And he died a very old man in Kashmir, where his tomb stands to this day.

    The book goes through every stage: royal descent from the Ariobarzanid dynasty; years of learning among the “white priests” in Egypt; preaching on the shores of the Bosphorus; the trial, Pilate's conspiracy and a carefully planned staging of the execution. The Shroud is read not as a relic but as a protocol of material analysis: the fabric, the pollen, the isotope trail of the water this man drank. Mary Magdalene is not a disciple but a queen: his wife, the mother of his children, the flight to Gaul and the birth of the Merovingians.

    And then — the operation that concealed all of it. Empress Helena gave the new religion its heart — Jerusalem. Eusebius of Caesarea gave it its mind and memory — canon and history. “Together they did not merely found a state Church. They accomplished the greatest substitution in history”. The second part of the book leads to Babylon — and to the question of why it was necessary to “move” Paradise.

    “Christ” is a job description written by the victors. “Radomir” is the name given to him by his parents at the dawn of the spring equinox. The difference between them is the difference between a living human being and a theological construct.

  • Shadmehr: Proof of the Real Name ♪
    About the book

    “A name is the first thing a man receives and the last thing taken from him.”

    Today two billion people pronounce a name that never existed. “Jesus Christ” is a Greek transliteration of a common Aramaic name and a ritual title, “the anointed one”. A job description. Not the name of a specific man from a specific lineage.

    This book is neither theology nor a dispute about faith. “We are not looking for theological truth. Not for revelation. Not for faith.” The investigation looks for one word — the word by which a man was called who was born in the first century in Cappadocia, into a family of Iranian origin, at the Spring Equinox.

    The answer is SHADMEHR, “the joy of Mithra”. SHAD — joy. MEHR — Mithra, the God of Light and Covenant: in the first century, for any educated inhabitant of Cappadocia, this word carried the name of the God. The town of SHADMEHR stands on the map of Iran today, in Razavi Khorasan Province, and living people bear the name. On another branch of the same language tree it sounds as RADOMIR. Not a borrowing — one name in two languages of one family, like the Russian mat’ and the English mother.

    Then the investigation follows the chronology of destruction. Three traditions kept the true name — the Iranian, the Proto-Indo-European (in its Proto-Slavic form) and the Gnostic. Five blows fell on them, centuries apart: Eusebius’s canon in the fourth century, Persia’s conquest by the Arabs in the seventh, the displacement of the runic system of images in the tenth, Peter the Great’s civil script in the eighteenth, and the spelling reform of 1918, which merged МИР, peace, and МIР, the Universe, into one word. “Five events. Each with its own cause. Its own agents. Its own motives. No coordination.” Yet the cumulative effect is as though someone had been methodically removing everything that might help one question to be asked.

    The system acted where it could: in texts. “But a root is not in a manuscript. A toponym is not in the canon. Pollen is not in a decree.” The book brings independent lines to one point: Strabo and the sanctuaries of Mithra in Cappadocia; the coins of the Ariobarzanids, carrying Iranian names right through the age of Hellenisation; the tombstone of Pantera of Sidon in the museum at Bad Kreuznach; the mother’s name, Maryam; and the physical data of the Shroud — pollen from the Bosphorus and from Cappadocia, silver of the Cappadocian-Byzantine mints, the isotopes of Bosphorus water.

That the name SHADMEHR was natural to the Cappadocian aristocracy of the first century — proven.   • The Education of Radomir ♪
    About the book

    “The question worth asking is simple: why is an educated child a heresy?”

    The official Gospels describe an adult man: thirty years old, the Jordan, the ministry begins. What came before is more than twenty years of silence. Childhood removed. Education removed. The Infancy Gospel of Thomas, with its educated boy, was declared heretical by Irenaeus of Lyon in 180 CE. “Because an educated child prompts the next question — where did he study?”

    This book answers it.

    The investigation begins with what cannot be removed. In a museum in the German town of Bad Kreuznach stands a gravestone: Tiberius Julius Abdes Pantera, archer from Sidon, first century. Three independent traditions, hostile to one another — Roman, Jewish and Arabic — preserved the same name for the father. Not Joseph. Pantera. Rule One of the book: “A Hostile Witness Is Worth More Than a Friendly One”. The Babylonian Talmud and the pagan philosopher Celsus wished to discredit this man — and both preserved Egypt.

    Egypt was no chance refuge. Herod was hunting the newborn heir of the Ariobarzanid line, and the family — Maryam, the steward Joseph and the infant — settled in the Egyptian Babylon, on the Nile, where the church of Abu Serga stands above an ancient crypt. Eight kilometers from Heliopolis, where, according to Strabo, Plato studied for thirteen years. “Plato arrived in Heliopolis already a formed philosopher and received a course of study. Radomir arrived at four. He did not receive a course; he received an upbringing.”

    The book reconstructs the program of the priestly school, designed for twenty years: the forty-two books of Hermes Trismegistus described by Clement of Alexandria, medicine, the astronomy of cycles, the teaching of the First Principle — Ra-Atum, who in John becomes the Logos: “In the beginning was the Word.” And the first lesson, before astronomy and medicine, the Instructions of Ptahhotep: “Do not be arrogant because of your knowledge”.

    Hence what would later be called miracles. King Abgar wrote to him: “I have heard that you heal without medicines or herbs.” Hence the parable in which the fisherman hears about fish and the philosopher hears about Logos. “It was not charisma. It was education.”

    Then Caesarea of Cappadocia: the return not of a traveler but of an heir. And a team assembled before Byzantium: Peter and Andrew, the sons of Zebedee, Matthew the tax collector, Simon the Zealot, Philip, Nathanael. Abgar offered him a city and asylum — he declined: “everything for which I was sent must be accomplished here.”

    The epilogue names what he said in Byzantium: “The First Principle is accessible to every person directly — without intermediary, without temple, without payment.” And the logion of Thomas: “The Kingdom is within you and outside you.”

    The book honestly separates physical evidence, documented testimony and reconstruction — and asks only one thing: “read to the end before objecting”.

  • The Beloved Magdalene ♪
    About the book

    “Ask anyone on the street who Mary Magdalene was, and the answer comes quickly: a sinner, a prostitute, a repentant woman weeping at the feet of Christ.”

    This image has a starting point. In the autumn of 591, Pope Gregory the First, in a single homily, fused three different women of the Gospels into one harlot. Three hundred years earlier, Hippolytus of Rome had called her Apostola Apostolorum — the Apostle to the Apostles. “This was not interpretation. It was substitution.” In 1969 Rome, with a quiet correction in the liturgical calendar, removed her identification with the sinner. Without explanation. “A half-restoration, sufficient to acknowledge an error, insufficient to reveal the truth.”

    The investigation restores what came before Gregory — clue by clue.

    Her name is not a toponym but a title. Migdal means tower. Radomir named his people by their function: Cephas — the Rock, Boanerges — the Sons of Thunder. In this sequence the tower is the final guardian, “the one who carries the archive when all else falls”. Papyrus 66, the earliest surviving complete manuscript of the Gospel of John, preserves traces of edits to her role as early as the turn of the second and third centuries. “This is not theory. It is palaeography”.

    Her position. Luke names her first, before Joanna, the wife of the manager of Herod's household; the women of the circle provided for the mission “out of their means”. The anointing oil cost about three hundred denarii a pound — only wealthy citizens could buy it. Magdalene was wealthy: she came from a great lineage of priests of southern France. The anointing was not a gesture of tenderness but “an act of priestly power”. And the “seven demons” are “the formula for completing an initiation, not a medical diagnosis”.

    Her speech is the language of an initiate. In the Gospel of Mary the soul ascends through the Powers, the last of which takes seven forms: the seven Powers of Wrath. In the Pistis Sophia, of one hundred and fifteen key questions, sixty-seven are asked by her and seven by Peter. Radomir tells her: “Mary, you are she who will be the inheritor of all the Kingdom of the Light.” “Inheritor — not disciple, not follower.”

    “He: the male branch, Cappadocia, Heliopolis. She: the female branch, Provence, Anahita.” “Their union was not a romantic story” — it was the joining of two priestly lines of one ancient tradition.

    Provence. A boat without oars or sails, a landing at the mouth of the Rhône — “not a group of refugees”. The cave of Sainte-Baume was a place of veneration before her, and the moment you enter, you see that “this is not a cell. It is a hall”. She spent thirty years there, and the book shows that “those thirty years were ministry, not penitence”. Then the interception: in 1279, thirty-five years after Montségur, Charles II of Anjou “discovered” the relics, and in 1295 they were handed to the Dominicans.     About the book

    In the Khaniyar quarter of old Srinagar stands Roza Bal. The name of the man buried there is Yuz Asaf, the leader of the healed. The tomb is first mentioned in 112 CE. The inner sarcophagus lies on an east-west axis — the common Indo-European rite; over it lies a tombstone oriented north-south. “Someone deliberately concealed the original rite beneath a Muslim one.” Beside the grave are stone footprints with marks; the custodians themselves call them the marks of the nails. “There is no history of crucifixion in Kashmir.”

    The book poses the question it must close before anything else: “Is this the same man?” And it answers not with faith but by comparison. The three-dimensional reconstruction from the Shroud: a height between 175 and 182 centimeters, broad shoulders, an Iranian-Anatolian facial type — the same as on the coins of the Ariobarzanids. Five descriptions from different eras, whose authors had no knowledge of one another. In Kashmir the dead were cremated, yet this man was buried — as an enlightened one or as a foreigner. At Lake Manasarovar, when King Shalivahana asks who he is, he answers: Son of God. And adds: “My work among foreigners is complete.” The book notes at once that the manuscripts of this text are late and takes it as evidence of a living tradition. “Not the words of a tired wanderer.   • Philip — Not an Apostle ♪
    About the book

    “This book is about a man who is not there.”

    Not one line in his own hand survives. No confession of faith. No tomb. In three of the four Gospels he is a line in a list. “And upon this man, who left nothing behind, history hung more than upon any of his companions: two different gospels under his name, a legendary Acts, a cult, a tomb, another man's daughters, and another man's death.”

    “Little from him — much under his name.” That is the book's first question; the last concerns the machine that produces such asymmetries.

    In John, Philip is alive. He is not called — he is found: the only one of the Twelve whom Radomir sought out himself and took with two words, “Follow me”. “That is not how you talk a stranger round. That is how you collect the man you came for.” Bethsaida was no fishing village but the polis of Julias, named from the house of Caesar: so say the coin, Pliny and Josephus. His name, “lover of horses”, the royal name of Macedon, Radomir did not change: it already carried what was needed. Both his lines rest on one Greek word, “enough”: two hundred denarii would not be enough; and in the upper room, “Lord, show us the Father, and it is enough for us”. A pragmatist. A doorkeeper. A translator between worlds.

    What was done to him — by the documents. He was merged with Philip the deacon, and the editing flowed one way: from deacon to Apostle. Another man's daughters, another man's tomb at Hierapolis, another man's death were stitched onto him. Eusebius printed incompatible versions of the daughters in neighbouring chapters — “a seam within a single binding”. By the 370s two different gospels circulated under his name. A whole nest was cut from the common memory: Nathanael, Cana, the wedding. A second-century testimony says he was no martyr.

    Why — the book lays a stone and a list side by side. The Temple barrier slab threatens death to a foreigner stepping towards the sanctuary. Among the Twelve, at the very entrance, stand two men with Greek names. “The wall that executes the foreigner — and a staff that holds the door open for him.” Philip alone makes no confession of faith. “There was nothing to erase — he did not confess, he served.” He asks to be shown the Father and hears: “Whoever has seen me has seen the Father”. For the book he is a man of the old faith: in the inner circle, a living refutation of the founding myth. “Such a man is not refuted. Such a man is erased”.

    The Hellenes came to him: “he does not read them the terms, does not check them for purity, does not take a payment”. “He opened, passed them on, and stepped aside.” Hence the reader's criterion: “the one who opens and steps aside is a guardian; the one who plants himself in the doorway and takes is a collector. A guardian serves the passage. A collector feeds on the passage.” The machine that works by the name erases guardians and hangs their names on the door where the till stands. The book invents no conspiracy: “A vector with no headquarters. A current with no pump.”

    The investigation finds “a guardian of the old world, posted at the door through which the message entered the world of the many”. He held it open and “departed without leaving a door to himself”. The book restores not his biography — no one wrote it down — but his office. “As long as the word guardian is remembered at the door, the till in the doorway stands there unlawfully.”

  • Mechanism ♪
    About the book

    “Every crime has a weapon, a place, a time, and executors.”

    “This is not a theological dispute — it is forensic investigation.” The question of this book is not who was substituted, but how. What instruments, what people, what sequence of actions held the substitution in place for seventeen centuries.

    The weapon is one word. The Greek σταυρός is translated as “cross” in every Bible and in every catechism. But an ordinary dictionary — Liddell & Scott, Thayer’s Greek Lexicon — says something else: an upright post, a stake, a pile. In Thucydides and Xenophon these are stakes and piles. In Josephus Flavius, posts of execution. Forensic expert Frederick Zugibe established that the angle of blood flow on the Shroud corresponds to vertically raised arms. N. A. Morozov, in his seven-volume work “Christ”, wrote “posting” instead of “crucifixion”. “Post — not cross. Place — not Jerusalem.”

    The place is a hill with a view of the harbor. Golgotha comes from the Aramaic Gulgoleth, “skull”. Xerolofos, the second hill of Constantinople, is literally “the dry hill”, the bare hill. One name in two languages. In early texts, ships in the harbor could be seen from the height of the instrument of execution. From Jerusalem to the nearest sea is about seventy kilometers. Directly beneath Xerolofos lies the Golden Horn bay.

    On this hill Constantine erected his column. Its foundation is nearly four times wider than the column itself. Sealed into the base: the Palladium, the Axe of Noah, the alabaster vessel of Mary Magdalene, “particles of the True Cross”. The book reads this list not as a catalog of relics, but as the protocol of a ritual consecration of a place. Constantine sealed memory in stone. He thought he was burying it. It turned out he preserved it.

    The executors are five. Constantine, the client. Eusebius of Caesarea, the ideological architect. Macarius, the director on the ground: he chose where to dig, and he determined which cross was the true one. Helena, the public face: while she was “discovering” holy sites in Jerusalem, Constantine was building the column in Constantinople. Judas Cyriacus, the prop of legitimization. All five were canonized. “The Jerusalem find was the alibi for the Constantinople wood.”

    A separate chapter tells what the operation did not manage to stop. In October, a year before the stauros, the last ship of the season carried the pregnant Magdalene and her elder son David away from Constantinople. The mouth of the Rhône, a helmsman with a pole, the cave of Sainte-Baume. Her daughter Sophia was born on the new land.

  • Alexander, Aristotle, Avesta ♪
    About the book

    “Why did the man who razed Thebes to the ground leave no garrison in Cappadocia?”

    334 BC. Alexander of Macedon crosses the Hellespont with a debt of 1,300 talents. Not a single creditor is named in the sources. At Thebes, 30,000 people are sold into slavery. Everywhere the same pattern—Lydia, Phrygia, Cilicia: a Macedonian strategos and a garrison. In March 333 BC he enters Mazaka, the capital of Cappadocia, leaves a local man, Sabiktas, in charge—and departs without a garrison. The only time in the entire campaign.

    This book is the investigation of one anomaly. It divides its own material into three tiers: facts, circumstantial evidence, the author’s reconstruction.

    The facts. In Cappadocia, Strabo describes fire temples where “the Magi keep the fire eternally burning”. By its Old Iranian etymology, the name Mazaka most likely means the City of the Wise Lord. The dynasty Alexander spared was of one blood with the Ariobarzanids.

    Aristotle educated Alexander from the age of thirteen. According to Mary Boyce, he held Zoroaster and the ancient order of the Magi in high esteem. Yet his own “Politics” states: “The barbarian and the slave are by nature one and the same.” The Avesta said the opposite: “One path—the path of Truth. All others are not paths.” The First Principle is accessible to every person without an intermediary. “Two texts. Two systems. They cannot coexist.”

    The Avesta is not a prayer book but an archive. Astronomy, medicine, the history of cycles—and a schedule. The Zoroastrian tradition awaits three Saoshyants. N. A. Morozov, the astronomer Michael Molnar and the author of the cycle—three methods, different centuries—arrive at one date: March 20, 6 BC. On that day the Moon passed before Jupiter in the constellation of Aries.

    The reconstruction: the Magi of Mazaka showed Alexander the calculation. After Cappadocia, the line of his actions bends. After Issus, the family of Darius are not hostages. At Susa, ninety officers marry Persian women. At Opis he tells the army: “I regard all men as my people.”

    Then: who wrote it all down. The daily journal of the campaign went to Ptolemy. Arrian, himself governor of Cappadocia, devoted one line to March 333. The only source contemporary with the events—the Babylonian Astronomical Diary from the temple of Marduk—contradicts Arrian. Three hundred tablets lie untranslated in the British Museum.

    The death of Alexander leaves three unexplained facts: there is no will, the body has never been found, and in the East a state arises under Menander I Soter, the Savior—a ruler of unknown origins.

    The book does not hide the limits of what is known: “What exactly happened in the fire temple of Mazaka—the sources are silent.” And it adds: “But we know the result.”

    “He was not a prophet. He was an intelligent man who received knowledge—and used it in the only way available to him. He left. And let the fires keep burning.”

  • Legitimacy ♪
    About the book

    The question of this book: “how was the substitution maintained for seventeen centuries? Through what mechanism? Who built it, who serviced it, who paid for it?”

    “The answer lies in the history of power. Not religion. Power.”

    The book is written as an investigation. Every claim rests on sources. Every interpretation is kept apart from fact: the reader always knows what has been proven and what is the author’s reconstruction.

    First, the man behind the project. Constantine: the son of an army officer and of Helena, about whom the sources disagree — wife or concubine. In 305 he is passed over. In 306 the troops at York proclaim him augustus — and he claims that the army “forced him”. Sol Invictus on his coins until 325. The execution of his son Crispus, then of his wife Fausta. Baptism only on his deathbed.

    Then, the man who wrote the history. Eusebius of Caesarea had been excommunicated at the Synod of Antioch for his Arian sympathies. He came to Nicaea, signed a creed that condemned his own position — and became the chief historian of the winning side. He also took part in the blackening of Licinius, the co-ruler whom Constantine promised to spare and had hanged the following year.

    Nicaea, 325. The council meets in the emperor’s summer palace. Constantine pays for the travel and accommodation of all participants. Only two of the 318 refuse to sign the final document. In 331 Constantine commissions Eusebius to produce fifty copies of the Scriptures. Before that, no single text called “the Bible” existed. Afterwards, everything that did not make it into those fifty copies became less authoritative. Not forbidden — simply unofficial. “We read a Bible compiled by a man who had been excommunicated for heresy.” After Nicaea, heresy becomes a state crime for the first time, and “whoever controls the definition of heresy controls everything”.

    Within twenty years — a single hierarchy from Britain to Mesopotamia. The Church of the Holy Sepulchre gives the system a physical place. “Three elements. Three instruments. One system.”

    The book then shows the scheme repeating. Charlemagne: illegitimate, the counselor Alcuin, a crown on Christmas Day 800. The “Donation of Constantine”: a forged document by which real lands changed hands. The Templars: creditors to kings, arrested simultaneously across France in 1307. “Every time: a wound. A narrative. A system. Every time: the public beneficiary dies. The institution remains.”

    And at the end, what this mechanism was guarding. Before Nicaea, different communities read different gospels: of Thomas, of Mary, of Philip. In them is a different Christ. “A man who teaches. Who speaks in riddles. Who has disciples of both sexes. Who demands not death, not sacrifice — but understanding.”

    “The mechanism described here belongs to no single era. It reproduces itself. That is what makes history not merely an archive of the past, but an instruction manual for the present.”

    The functions of the goddesses did not disappear — they were shifted onto the man: consolation, mercy, keeping the hearth. And his own — the axis, form, the protection of the living, the direct fatherhood of meaning — was taken over by the system and redefined: “not service but duty; not the protection of the living but the seizure of what belongs to another; not the master but the soldier.”

    “Imagine a smith forced to spin while the smithy grows cold.”

    The question of Roman law — cui bono, who benefits — the book asks not with suspicions but with a balance sheet. “Who benefits? The one who sells intermediation.” The father of the family lost his household priesthood; the man lost his work, his circle, his tears. Then comes Domostroy — read, not retold. The dying Florentine Lorenzo Alberti, for whom the main work of his life was to bring his sons up into worthy men. The men’s circle, held together not by face-to-face conversation but by common work — shoulder to shoulder. And a separate chapter on what she lost in this castling.

    “The man was not elevated. The man was repurposed. His own work was taken from him — and another’s burden was laid on him.”

    The goal was neither the man nor the woman. “The goal was the meeting. So that it would not happen.”

    The last chapter is not advice. “Advice is given to a stranger. To one of your own — you remind.” Remember your body. Your work. Your shoulder. That you are a father. That you can bless. Remember her. “To bring back the man means to bring back the bank to the woman.”

    Give the Man Back is not a slogan. It is the second half of the same plea.

    The trilogy Driven Apart is read in order: Give the Women Back, Give the Man Back, Parental Blessing. At the heart of these books is the human being: they give a person back the memory of who they are. The spirit of the Father is in everyone, and a person repays their debt with love, not with sacrifice.

  • Parental Blessing
    About the book

    “From limb of my limb you are born. From my heart you are born. You are myself, called son. Live a hundred autumns.”

    So, three thousand years ago, a father spoke over a newborn in the ancient Indian birth rite. He breathed on its forehead — as a mother cow sniffs her calf. Three times he said into its ear: “Speech, speech, speech”. And then he gave the child to the mother — to her breast.

    Today a blessing is tossed out at weddings as a courtesy. This book tests the opposite: once it was the most powerful act one person could perform upon another. “It is a word that does what it says.”

    A blessing placed three things into a person: belonging, strength, place. And it was not one person who blessed — two did. The mother gives belonging: you are from my body, you are wanted. The father gives name, place and direction: here is your lineage, here is where you are growing, become, surpass. Two below — in the image of the two above: Sky-Father and Earth-Mother, from Ireland to India.

    How real was it? Real enough to be stolen. Jacob puts on goatskins, blind Isaac blesses the wrong son — and cannot take it back. Esau weeps: “Hast thou but one blessing, my father? bless me, even me also, O my father.” In Rus’ a child asked from an early age: “Mama, I’m going to sleep, bless me.” And the people said: “A parent’s blessing does not burn in fire or sink in water”.

    This is not superstition. It is a technology. The book breaks the rite down gesture by gesture — and asks science. A mother’s scent synchronizes the infant’s brain: in 2021 this was recorded with dual EEG. A mother’s voice lights up several systems in a child’s brain at once, even when the words are meaningless. Slow touch releases oxytocin and lowers cortisol. A meta-analysis of 169 studies: parental expectation causally changes a child’s destiny. And the hand rests on the crown — where an infant’s fontanelle is open, “a little spring.” Every claim is labeled: proven, reconstruction or tradition.

    Then it was taken away. “As long as the lineage feeds itself, the intermediary is unnecessary.” The book shows from the documents how, one after another, the lineage’s threads were taken from it: birth, kinship, marriage, inheritance. The decree Tametsi of 1563 made the presence of the parish priest necessary for a valid marriage. The gestures stayed the same — a hand on the head, the word, the anointing of the crown. “This is substitution in its purest form: leave the gesture, change the hand and the recipient.”

    “First take away—then offer yourself.”

    The last chapter is about how to restore it. Not in theory — with your hands. Begin with yourself: “the unblessed will not bless.” Bless yourself — for the child to whom this was never said. Then bless your own child: a hand on the crown, aloud, every day while the child is small, and at every threshold of life. “The chain severed thousands of years ago can be joined again. And you can join it. With your own hands.”

    The trilogy Driven Apart is read in order: Give the Women Back, Give the Man Back, Parental Blessing. At the heart of these books is the human being: they give a person back the memory of who they are. The spirit of the Father is in everyone, and a person repays their debt with love, not with sacrifice.
